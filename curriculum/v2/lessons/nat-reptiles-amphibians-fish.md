## Different body plans

Mammals stand with their legs under the body. Lizards, crocodiles and salamanders sprawl: the upper limb bones stick out sideways, the elbow and knee bend downward, and the body hangs low between the legs. Snakes have no limbs and move by bending the spine into S curves. Frogs have short bodies, no visible neck, and long hind legs folded in three sections that open like a spring. Fish are shaped by water: streamlined bodies with fins attached as thin planes. Start each drawing by naming which plan you are drawing.

## Build from the spine

For reptiles and amphibians, draw the spine first as one flowing line from snout to tail tip, then hang the body masses on it: a head wedge, a chest, a belly section, and a tail that tapers steadily. Attach the limbs at the shoulder and hip as sideways tubes, bend them down at the elbow and knee, and splay the feet. For a snake, keep the width changing smoothly, thickest in the middle third of the body, with a distinct head and a long taper to the tail; draw the S curve in perspective so that near loops are larger and overlap far ones. For a frog, build the hind leg as thigh, shin and long foot folded against each other, and track each joint from several angles.

## Fish as cross-sections

A fish is easier to draw once you think about its cross-section. A tuna is nearly round in section, a flatfish is very flat, and many reef fish are tall and thin. Draw a side view, then a head-on view of the same fish; the cross-section tells you how its surface turns. Fins are thin planes attached along lines on the body. Draw where each fin attaches before drawing its outline, and let the fins overlap the body in perspective.

## Scales and skin

Scales are rows of overlapping shapes that follow the surface like contour lines. Draw a few guide contours around the body first, then lay the scales along them, smaller and more crowded where the surface turns away. Do not cover the whole body: render scales near the edge of the light side and around the focal area, and let them fade into plain tone elsewhere. The same applies to the texture of frog skin and the plates on a turtle shell.

## Reference

Use specimen photos and historical natural-history plates, and draw live animals at an aquarium, a pet shop or a garden pond when you can.
