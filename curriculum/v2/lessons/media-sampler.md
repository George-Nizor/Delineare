## How to use the sampler

Each medium has its own handling, but the same three tests show how it behaves: a value scale, a highlight made by lifting tone or by keeping the paper clean, and one small study of a simple object. Try one medium at a time, on paper that suits it, and write three lines on what it made easy and what it made hard. Nothing else in the curriculum depends on this module.

## Graphite and charcoal

Graphite grades run from hard (H, pale and fine) to soft (B, dark and grainy); HB, 2B and 6B cover most needs. Build darks in layers with a softer grade rather than pressing hard, because heavy pressure flattens the paper's tooth into a shiny surface that will not take more tone. Charcoal comes as vine or willow sticks, which are soft, pale and easy to wipe away, and as compressed sticks or pencils, which are darker and harder to remove. Work charcoal from large light masses toward darker accents, and lift highlights by pressing a kneaded eraser onto the tone rather than rubbing. Charcoal smudges easily: rest your hand on a clean sheet and store drawings with paper between them. If you use fixative, test it on a scrap first, spray outdoors or somewhere well ventilated, and use light coats, since it can darken the values.

## Ink and ink wash

Ink is permanent once dry, so plan with a light pencil guide and build tone by hatching, cross-hatching or stippling. For a wash, dilute ink with water in separate cups to make a value scale, test each on scrap paper, and work from light to dark, because a dark wash cannot be lightened. Keep the white of the paper for the lightest lights, and check whether your pen ink is waterproof before laying a wash over the lines.

## Colour media

Coloured pencil is built in light layers; heavy pressure early seals the paper so later layers slide off. Alcohol markers blend only while wet, so work quickly and in one direction. Watercolour is transparent: plan from light to dark and leave bare paper for highlights. Gouache is opaque and its values shift slightly as it dries, so test swatches first. Soft pastel sits between drawing and painting; use light pressure early so the paper's tooth can hold later layers, and keep the dust away from your face.
