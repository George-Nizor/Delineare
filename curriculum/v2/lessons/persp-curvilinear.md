## The idea

Ordinary linear perspective assumes a flat picture plane, and it works well for views up to about 60 degrees wide. Spread it wider and objects near the edges of the picture stretch badly. Curvilinear perspective avoids this by projecting the scene onto a curved surface and then flattening it, which turns most straight lines into curves. The best-known version is five-point perspective: four vanishing points spaced evenly on a circle around the view, plus one at the centre, giving a fisheye look that covers about half of everything around you.

## Which lines stay straight

In a fisheye drawing, any straight line that passes through the centre of the view stays straight on the page. Lines that do not pass through the centre curve, and they curve more the further they are from it. A set of parallel edges across the view becomes a family of arcs that meet at two opposite vanishing points on the circle, and edges that point straight away from you all converge on the centre point. So in a corridor seen dead centre, the floor and ceiling lines running away from you stay straight and meet in the middle, while the walls' vertical edges bow outward.

## Building a five-point grid by hand

Draw a large circle and mark points on its rim at top, bottom, left and right, plus one at the centre. Draw the horizon and the vertical through the centre as straight lines. Then draw arcs that pass through the left and right points; they bulge more the further they are from the horizon, like lines of longitude on a globe seen from the side. Do the same between the top and bottom points. Lines from the centre to the rim are straight spokes. A box placed in the middle looks almost normal; the same box near the rim bends and leans toward the circle.

## When to use it

Use curvilinear perspective when the picture needs a very wide view, such as a cramped room seen from a corner, a skate bowl, or a crowd seen from directly above, or when the bending itself is the point, for disorientation or comedy. For ordinary scenes, a normal or moderately wide view reads better. Draw the same room three ways (normal, wide and fisheye) and compare how each feels.

While you learn, a curvilinear grid built by hand, or a fisheye assistant in drawing software such as Krita's, keep the curves honest. Then try the same layouts by hand without the grid.
