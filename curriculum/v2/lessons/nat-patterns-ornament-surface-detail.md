## Patterns are rules

A pattern is a motif repeated by a rule: along a line (a border), across a grid (a tile or a textile), around a centre (a rosette) or along a spiral. The rule matters more than the motif. Before drawing, decide the unit (one leaf, one scroll, one triangle), the spacing, and how each repeat relates to the last one: shifted, mirrored, rotated or scaled. Rule the guide structure lightly, whether that is a grid, a set of circles or a centre line, and keep the spacing even. Uneven spacing reads as a mistake, not as variety.

## Sources

Historical ornament books and natural forms are the two great sources. Old ornament plates show how different cultures turned plants and geometry into repeats; leaves, shells, seed heads and the microscopic skeletons in natural-history plates show symmetry and growth spirals. Copy a plate to learn its rule, then make a new pattern with that rule and a motif of your own. A shell's spiral can become a border; the veins of a leaf can become a branching filler.

## Wrapping a pattern around a form

On a curved object a pattern must follow the surface. Draw contour lines around the form first, such as rings on a cylinder or lines of latitude and longitude on a sphere, and use them as the pattern's grid. Units on the part of the surface facing you keep their full width; units near the edge, where the surface turns away, compress in the direction of the turn. Stripes on a cylinder follow its ellipses, and a checkerboard on a sphere shrinks and bends toward the silhouette.

## Density and emphasis

A pattern at one scale and one density everywhere flattens a design. Vary the scale between a large motif and a small filler, and make the pattern denser where you want attention and sparser elsewhere. On clothing, armour or a creature's hide, let the pattern fade in shadow and toward the edges of the form, so it supports the form instead of sitting on top of it.
