## Stylisation is selection

Stylising means deciding what to keep, what to simplify and what to push. A realistic drawing keeps most of what the eye sees; a stylised one keeps the few things that carry the character and turns the rest into clear, simple shapes. The construction underneath does not go away: a cartoon head is still a volume that turns in space, and its features still sit on its surface.

## Exaggeration with control

Exaggeration works by enlarging a difference that is already there. Find the one or two features that define a face or a character, such as a long jaw, a big nose, small close-set eyes or a heavy brow, and push those while keeping the rest close to neutral. Push in steps (a quarter more, half as much again, double) and check at each step that it is still recognisably the same character; past a certain point it becomes someone else. Exaggerate every feature equally and the result looks generic, because the relationships that made the face particular disappear.

## Levels of simplification

Draw one character at three levels: realistic, semi-stylised and minimal. At each step remove detail and merge small shapes into larger ones, but keep the proportions and silhouette that carry the personality. Write down what you removed and what you kept. If the minimal version still reads as the same character, you have found the shapes that matter.

## Cartoon construction

Classic cartoon characters are built from simple volumes: a ball or pear for the head and body, a guide line wrapping the head for the eyes, a centre line for the turn. Draw them many times, from different angles, as solid forms before adding features. Squash and stretch, where a volume changes shape while keeping its size, makes them feel flexible without losing their structure.

## Caricature and respect

A caricature of a real person exaggerates that person's own proportions. Practise on portraits from public-domain museum collections, and do not make mocking caricatures of private individuals.
