## Why still life

A still life holds still, keeps its light and lets you take as long as you need, so it is where measuring, block-in and checking come together. Set it up once and keep it: one lamp to the side, a plain background, objects of different heights that overlap a little, and your seat marked so you return to the same view each session.

## Life and photo are different references

A photograph is flat. The camera has one eye, so the slight differences between your two eyes and the shifts you get from small head movements are gone, and you must infer form from shading alone. Phone lenses also distort at close range: a wide lens close to the subject enlarges whatever is nearest, so a mug photographed from 40 cm has a bigger rim and a more stretched shape than the same mug seen from your seat further back. Photos compress the range of tones, pushing shadows toward black and lights toward white, and the camera chooses the exposure for you. In return, a photo stays still, can be enlarged, measured and overlaid, and lets you check proportions precisely, which life never allows.

From life, your eyes and head movements tell you which edges turn away, the shadow side keeps information a photo loses, and you must choose what to measure because nothing holds still for an overlay. That makes life the better trainer for seeing form, and a photo the better tool for checking proportion.

## A fair comparison

To see the difference yourself, draw the same set-up both ways. Photograph it from your drawing position, with the lens at your eye height and from the distance you sit, not closer. Draw from the photo for twenty minutes and from life for twenty minutes, in either order. Overlay the two drawings and write three differences. Typically the photo drawing has flatter shadows, harder outlines and slightly different proportions near the frame edges.

## Rules for drawing from photos

Use your own photos or openly licensed ones. Display the photo at about the size you draw, not zoomed in on a detail. Take photos from at least two metres away and zoom in rather than walking up close, which avoids the near-object distortion. Check the drawing against the photo by overlay before you shade. And keep life in the mix: alternate the two, so the flat look of a photo does not become the look of your drawings.
