## What a likeness is made of

A likeness depends more on the big proportions of one particular head than on how well the features are rendered. The outer shape of the head, the hairline, the width of the face compared with its length, where the eyes sit and how far apart, the length of the nose, and the gaps from nose to mouth to chin all differ from person to person, and a viewer recognises them before any detail. A beautifully drawn eye in the wrong place loses the likeness; a rough head with the right proportions keeps it.

## A routine: map, then build

Start with a proportion map. On the photo, or by sighting from life, find the eye line, the base of the nose, the mouth line and the chin, and compare their spacing with the width of the face. Choose one unit, such as the distance from the eye line to the chin, and write the other measurements as fractions of it. Then build the head as a construction at the right angle and place those lines on it before drawing any feature. Block in the hairline, jaw, brow and cheek planes as shapes. Only then place the features, and check them against the map. Flip the drawing and the photo every ten minutes to see errors you have stopped noticing, and overlay at 50 percent before you shade.

## Find what is particular

Name three proportions that make this face different from an average one: perhaps a long gap from nose to mouth, wide-set eyes and a narrow jaw. Write them beside the drawing. If you push those three by about a fifth in a quick sketch and it looks more like the person, you have found the right features. Most faces are less symmetrical than we draw them, and most drawings make the eyes too high and too large.

## Choosing reference

Use photos taken from at least two metres away and zoomed in, at eye level, with one main light. Close phone selfies distort the face: the nose grows and the ears shrink back. Self-portraits from a mirror are good practice because the model is always available; mark where your head goes so it stays in the same place. Copying portrait drawings from open-access collections shows how other artists simplified the same problems.
