## 1. Method steps, numbers, rules and mistakes, by source

### Drawabox, "Ellipses" theory page (DB-E)

What an ellipse stands for:

> "This page explores ellipses, a shape that is commonly used to represent circles that are rotated
> in 3D space, the properties that define them, and how those properties change based on the circles
> they're intended to represent in three dimensions."

> "For our purposes here in this course, we're going to be using ellipses in 2D space (drawn on the
> page) to represent circles that exist in 3D space."

Degree (numbers: 90 and 0):

> "When the circle (in 3D space) is facing the viewer, it's going to be at its widest - 90 degrees.
> This is the same as drawing a circle on the page. As the 3D circle turns away from the viewer, the
> ellipse representing it will get narrower, until finally it reaches a degree of 0, when the ellipse
> is facing away."

Degree shift (probably beyond lesson 2; belongs with lessons 9, 10, 14):

> "Throughout this course, we'll refer to this concept as the "degree shift" - basically the idea that
> the degree of our ellipse will shift wider or narrower, depending on how it moves in space."

Minor axis and major axis:

> "There's a major axis as well which runs perpendicular to it (across the widest span), but it's the
> minor axis that is actually the useful component."

Normal vector (beyond lesson 2; useful for lesson 14):

> "This means that we've got two ways of establishing the orientation of our circle in 3D space, as we
> draw it on our 2D page:
> - The degree (width) of the ellipse controlling the circle's rotation
> - The minor axis of the ellipse controlling the orientation of the circle's normal vector - basically
>   the direction that the circle is facing."

Homework quantities and medium:

> "The homework assignment for this section is as follows:
> - 2 filled pages of the Tables of Ellipses exercise
> - 2 filled pages of the Ellipses in Planes exercise
> - 1 filled page of the Funnels exercise
>
> All the assigned work for this section should be done in ink, using fineliners/felt tip pens as
> described here. In a pinch, I will accept work done in ballpoint, but only if the situation is dire.
> This is an exception only for this lesson as students get started."

Video on the page: "Drawabox Lesson 1: Ellipses", 12:48 (https://youtu.be/tHJ3rzk6kno). Its
description says it is "accompanying material for the text available here:
https://drawabox.com/lesson/1/5". It covers this theory page, not the exercises.

### Drawabox, "Tables of Ellipses" (DB-T)

The idea (a target set before drawing):

> "The first exercise is relatively straight forward, and involves drawing a lot of ellipses. These are
> not, however, simply free ellipses with no real goals. Instead, it is pinned on the idea of setting
> out criteria and targets for the ellipses we intend to draw, before drawing them. Therefore, when you
> draw your ellipse, it is either correct, or it isn't."

Steps, in page order:

> "Start off by taking your piece of paper and dividing it into a table with two columns and a bunch of
> rows."

> "Each of these sections will contain a different variation on the exercise - here you can experiment
> with different approaches, but here's a few that you can try out first."

> "For this one, you draw a circle starting from the far left of the box. Then, draw another beside it.
> Keep repeating it until you fill in the whole box. Strive to make your circles touch the top and
> bottom of the box, as well as the line to the left of it."

> "Next, same idea, but with ellipses. Within the same section, you should aim to draw ellipses of the
> same degree. You can also play with the angle of the ellipse, and this should also be consistent
> within the same section."

> "This one's a little different. Draw a wave through the section, dividing it into irregular pockets
> of space. Then fill these spaces with circles or ellipses, trying to keep them touching the bounds of
> the section as well as the curve. Everything should fit in there snugly, and nothing should be
> floating around."

Purpose (this is where the visible checks come from):

> "This exercise is meant to get you used to drawing ellipses, in a variety of sizes, orientations and
> degrees. It also sets out a clear space each ellipse is meant to occupy, giving us a means to assess
> whether or not an ellipse was successful, or if there were visible mistakes (where it went outside of
> its allotted space, or ended up falling short)."

Things to remember:

> "Draw confidently - use the ghosting method and "draw through" your ellipses two full times before
> lifting your pen to achieve a smooth, even shape."

> "Always set out a goal for the ellipse you're about to draw. This is generally what the "planning"
> phase of the ghosting method is for."

Mistake: drawing without a concrete goal:

> "I've seen lots of people do these in the past - that is, drawing ellipses floating inside of other
> ellipses. This is my opinion of course, but I don't think they're terribly useful, since they don't
> give a concrete target to aim for."

Mistake: not drawing through (the best wrong-and-right pair for this lesson):

> "This is something I'm pretty adamant about - you should be drawing through every single ellipse you
> draw for my lessons. That is, draw around the ellipse two or three times before lifting your pen. Two
> is ideal in my opinion, but three is also acceptable."

> "When you try to hit your ellipse in a single round, it's usually going to come out uneven and wobbly
> (due to drawing too slowly and carefully) or extremely loose in relation to the space in which it is
> meant to fit (due to simply not having built up the muscle memory to nail an ellipse accurately).
> Drawing through your ellipses gives your arm the chance to familiarize itself with what's being asked
> of it in that first pass, and then firm it up in the second."

> "Along with giving you an extra chance to build up the muscle memory, it also helps you maintain the
> confidence needed to achieve a smooth, even shape, without totally losing control."

> "As you get better, your ellipses will tighten up - the gaps between your successive passes will
> shrink and eventually your ellipses will appear much cleaner."

Example homework: "Your end result will look something like this." (an image; the writer should look
at it on the page before drawing the diagram).

Videos linked from the page: "Drawabox Lesson 1, Exercise 4: Tables of Ellipses", 6:36
(https://youtu.be/7WLmXufShyA), and "Drawabox Lesson 1, Exercise 4: Things to Remember (Tables of
Ellipses)", 5:56 (https://youtu.be/gyRHkTPqfrQ).

### Drawabox, "Ellipses in Planes" (DB-P)

Needs the pages from Ghosted Planes (DB-GP), which Delineare's lesson 1 does not teach.

> "Put an ellipse in there. That's all."

> "Don't worry about anything aside from getting the ellipse to be smooth and evenly shaped, and having
> it touch all four edges of the plane as snugly as you can manage. Of course, draw through your
> ellipses (as mentioned in the table of ellipses exercise)."

Priorities, in order:

> "The purpose of this exercise however is just about maintaining the smooth, even shape above all
> else. That is your first priority. Second to that is, as mentioned above, fitting it snugly within
> the plane, touching all four edges."

> "More than anything, it's an exercise in calming down."

Mistake: deformed ellipse:

> "See how the ellipse is bumpy and wobbly, and it kind of reaches out to touch the edges rather than
> maintaining an evenness to its shape? Avoid this. As mentioned several times above, maintaining a
> smooth elliptical shape is critical and above all else is your first priority."

Mistake: floating ellipse:

> "While getting the ellipse to touch all four edges is your second priority, it is still something you
> should strive to do. It's pretty clear that with this example, no attempt was made to achieve that
> goal, and the ellipse - though evenly shaped - was just plopped in the middle rather
> unceremoniously."

### Drawabox, "Funnels" (DB-F)

> "Start out by drawing a long line with a shorter one across it marking out its middle (roughly). This
> long line is going to be the minor axis we use to align the ellipses we draw later."

> "It's worth mentioning that you should use a ruler or straight edge to draw these two lines. The
> ellipses will be freehand, and we want to focus on one thing at a time."

> "Along either side of the long line, draw an arc. [...] Alternatively, if you can find a large
> circular object, or something else to help you draw those curves, by all means, go ahead. Again,
> we're not practicing our freehand curves right now."

> "In the space inside of the funnel, draw your ellipses. Strive to keep them aligned to the central
> minor axis line, such that each ellipse is cut into two equal, symmetrical halves down their narrower
> dimension by it."

> "Optionally, you can try to get the degree of your ellipses to increase as you move outwards from the
> center - keeping the middle one at a low degree (very narrow), and towards the outside, much more
> circular. Again, this is optional - you don't need to worry about this if you're still struggling
> with the alignment of your ellipses."

Mistakes:

> "Mistake: Not aligned. This is easily the most common mistake I see in this exercise. [...] You want
> to make sure that the minor axis cuts each ellipse into two equal, symmetrical halves, down their
> narrower dimension. Here you can clearly see that they're slanted and cut unevenly."

> "Mistake: Poor spacing. Keep your ellipses snugly against one another - don't leave large spaces
> between them (or any space at all for that matter)."

> "Mistake: Being too loose. Do your best to keep your ellipses snugly within the funnel shape. Here many
> of these ellipses are spilling out beyond the funnel."

## 4. Terms, in the sources' own words

- **Ellipse.** "a shape that is commonly used to represent circles that are rotated in 3D space" (DB-E).
- **Degree.** "This width (always measured across the narrowest span that passes through the center of
  the ellipse) is referred to as the "degree" - because it is tied to the angle of rotation (in degrees)
  of the circle itself, relative to the viewer." (DB-E). A circle is 90, a line is 0 (see the 90/0
  quote above).
- **Minor axis.** "We call the line that passes across the ellipse's narrowest span, and through its
  center, its minor axis. This minor axis line will cut the ellipse into two equal, symmetrical halves,
  so if it were to be cut out of the page and folded in half across this line, it would match up
  perfectly." (DB-E)
- **Major axis.** "a major axis as well which runs perpendicular to it (across the widest span)" (DB-E)
- **Drawing through.** "draw around the ellipse two or three times before lifting your pen" (DB-T);
  "drawn through two full times before lifting your pen" (DB-FAQ).
- **Ghosting.** "Ghosting is the act of going through the motion of drawing a mark, but without
  actually touching the page." (DB-GL; already defined in lesson 1)
- **Degree shift** and **normal vector**: defined in DB-E (quoted in section 1). Both are more than
  lesson 2 needs.

## 5. Proposal (not source text; every element cites its quote)

### Steps (5)

1. **Rule the table.** Divide the page "into a table with two columns and a bunch of rows" (DB-T). A
   ruler is allowed for the table; Drawabox rules its guide lines in Funnels ("use a ruler or straight
   edge to draw these two lines. The ellipses will be freehand", DB-F).
2. **Set the target.** Before each ellipse, choose where it must touch: the top and bottom of the row
   and the ellipse before it ("Strive to make your circles touch the top and bottom of the box, as well
   as the line to the left of it", DB-T; "Always set out a goal for the ellipse you're about to draw",
   DB-T). For an ellipse row, pick one degree and one angle and keep them for the row ("same degree",
   "consistent within the same section", DB-T).
3. **Ghost it.** Turn the page to a comfortable angle and ghost the loop from the shoulder ("rotate the
   page to find a comfortable angle of approach for each mark", DB-FAQ; "that includes ellipses",
   DB-GL; "engaging your whole arm from the shoulder", DB-FAQ).
4. **Draw through twice.** Put the pen down and go round two full times without stopping, then lift
   ("two full times before lifting your pen", DB-T and DB-FAQ). Keep the pen moving through the narrow
   ends ("not stop at the ends, otherwise they may became points", DIS-34).
5. **Leave it.** Do not go back over a miss; plan the next one ("Do not correct your mistakes", DB-FAQ).
   This matches lesson 1's "Leave it" step.

Optional sixth step, if the writer wants degree named in the lesson: try one row of circles, one of
narrow ellipses and one of wide ellipses (DB-T's three variations; DB-E's 90 to 0 range).

### The usual mistake (one pair)

- **Wrong:** one slow, careful loop that wobbles, or that lands loose in its space.
- **Right:** a fast loop drawn through twice; the second pass firms up the first.
- **Reason, from DB-T:** "When you try to hit your ellipse in a single round, it's usually going to come
  out uneven and wobbly (due to drawing too slowly and carefully) or extremely loose [...]. Drawing
  through your ellipses gives your arm the chance to familiarize itself with what's being asked of it in
  that first pass, and then firm it up in the second."

Second candidate if the first is too close to lesson 1: the deformed ellipse that "reaches out to touch
the edges rather than maintaining an evenness to its shape" (DB-P). Smooth beats snug.

### Main exercise

- **What:** a table of ellipses: two columns, rows of circles, rows of same-degree ellipses, and at
  least one row split by a wave and filled (DB-T).
- **How many:** two filled pages (DB-E homework: "2 filled pages of the Tables of Ellipses exercise").
- **How long:** no Drawabox page gives a time. The only sourced figure is DIS-34's "at least 20" ellipses
  in "10 minutes" (or "15", the page disagrees with itself). Do not invent a page time; either use
  "at least 20 ellipses, 10 to 15 minutes" from DIS-34 or leave time out and time a test run (WRITING.md
  step 5).
- **With what:** ballpoint pen or fineliner, matching lesson 1. Drawabox wants fineliners and accepts
  ballpoint "only if the situation is dire" (DB-E); DIS-34 uses "pencil – any" with an eraser. Ink
  keeps the "do not correct" rule honest.

### Checks (5)

1. Every ellipse is smooth, with no bumps or flat spots ("smooth and evenly shaped"; "bumpy and wobbly"
   is the mistake, DB-P).
2. Each ellipse touches the top and bottom of its row and its neighbour ("touch the top and bottom of
   the box, as well as the line to the left of it", DB-T).
3. No ellipse goes past its space or stops short of it ("where it went outside of its allotted space, or
   ended up falling short", DB-T).
4. The ellipses in one row match in width and tilt ("same degree", "consistent within the same section",
   DB-T).
5. Each ellipse shows two passes lying close together ("two full times", DB-FAQ; "the gaps between your
   successive passes will shrink", DB-T).

As in lesson 1, Drawabox ranks smoothness before fit (DB-P). The checks can be presented as levels the
same way.

### Practise later (4)

1. **Tables of ellipses** · as a warm-up, part of "two or three exercises [...] 10-15 minutes (total)"
   (DB-FAQ).
2. **Funnels** · one page: rule a long axis and a short cross line, draw two arcs (ruler or a round
   object allowed), fill the funnel with ellipses split evenly by the axis (DB-F; "1 filled page",
   DB-E). Good bridge to lessons 10 and 14, since the page says these principles "are used heavily in
   lesson 2" (Drawabox's lesson 2 is sausages with contour lines, Delineare's lesson 10).
3. **Ellipses in planes** · needs ghosted planes first: four dots, the four edges, the two diagonals and
   the cross (DB-GP), then one ellipse touching all four edges (DB-P; "2 filled pages", DB-E).
4. **Circles, round and round** · at least 20 circles, going round several times light and fast (DIS-32).
   Note the conflict below before using it.

### Where the sources disagree

- **How many passes.** DB-T's mistake section: "two or three times [...] Two is ideal in my opinion, but
  three is also acceptable." DB-T's own "Things to remember" and DB-FAQ: "two full times", "no less, no
  more". DIS-32 (circles): "go around multiple times", "start light and fast", then darken. Proposal
  uses two, the stricter and more recent-sounding Drawabox rule.
