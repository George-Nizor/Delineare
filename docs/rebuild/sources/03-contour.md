## 1. Method steps, numbers, rules and mistakes, by source

### Ctrl+Paint, "Drawing Shape: Contour" (CP, CP-V)

Page text, complete:

> "There's a variety of ways to describe form with a pencil, and the most common is 'contour'.  This is
> a linear approach to drawing, focusing on creating descriptive outlines.  The trick is to draw the
> unique contour you're observing, and not a generalized outline drawn from your memory.  Beginners
> often fall into the trap of drawing 'symbols' instead of actual objects -- even if they are sitting
> down with an object directly in front of them.  As children we're taught to draw symbols, and
> becoming an artist requires you critically look and record."

The page is filed under "Ctrl-Paint Unplugged" (pencil on paper), posted November 8, 2011.

The author, replying in the page's comments (signed "Matt Kohr"):

> "I agree, it's unusual to be able to draw in perfect contour without any structure underneath ---
> but this exercise is really important for careful observation.
> Really, it's more about learning to see :)"

Video transcript (YouTube auto-captions, no punctuation; quoted as captioned, with timestamps). The
symbol:

> screwdriver because when i drew 0:16 this i didn't have any reference so this is my generalized sense
> of all the screwdrivers i've ever seen as a 0:24 result it's kind of boring"

> 0:32 "drawing is looking at not a generalized object but the actual object in front of you and that
> it's going to have a very 0:41 specific and unique shape"

### Wikipedia, "Blind contour drawing" (WP)

> "Blind contour drawing is a drawing exercise, where an artist draws the contour of a subject without
> looking at the paper. The artistic technique was introduced by Kimon Nicolaïdes in The Natural Way to
> Draw, and it is further popularized by Betty Edwards as "pure contour drawing" in The New Drawing on
> the Right Side of the Brain."

Technique:

> "The student fixes their eyes on the outline of the model or object, then tracks the edge of the
> object with their eyes, while simultaneously drawing the contour very slowly, in a steady, continuous
> line without lifting the pencil or looking at the paper."

Why (note the right-brain claim, which Delineare has dropped; see R03 and section 5):

> "Nicolaïdes instructs students to keep the belief that the pencil point is actually touching the
> contour. He suggested that the technique improves students' drawings because it causes students to
> use both senses of sight and touch. Edwards suggests that pure contour drawing creates a shift from
> left mode to right mode thinking. [...] Blind contour drawing may not produce a lifelike drawing;
> however, it helps students to draw more realistically, rather than relying on their memorized drawing
> symbols. Blind contour drawing trains the eye and hand to work as a team, and it helps students to see
> all of the details of the object."

> "Some artists use contour drawing to warm up for a drawing session."

### Arts Impact, "Lesson One: Contour Lines" (AI)

A fourth-grade lesson plan built on the same subject as Delineare's exercise: the learner's own shoe.
Two drawings: a looked contour, then a blind one.

Contour line drawing, the tips:

> "Oftentimes, an artist doing a contour line drawing won't pick up his/her pencil for the whole
> drawing."

> "Here are two tricks that help you do a great contour line drawing:
> a. Look at the thing you are drawing MUCH more than at your paper.
> b. Focus your vision on the top of your object, and put your pencil at the top of your paper. Then
> move your eyes slowly down the object while your drawing hand moves at the same speed."

> "Take off one of your shoes and put it in an interesting position on your desk. Remember a contour
> line is a truth-telling line. Only draw what you see."

Losing concentration:

> "If you notice that you have lost your concentration at some point, go back to the place on your
> drawing where you can see that you were still concentrating, and just start drawing again from that
> point. Don't bother to erase. Extra lines add a feeling of life to a drawing."

The blind drawing:

> "Now, we're going to do a second contour line drawing of our shoes (placed in a new position), but
> we're not going to look at our drawings until we are done."

> "This is called a blind contour line drawing, and it's a way artists strengthen their looking skills.
> You may be surprised to discover that your blind contour line drawing is more accurate in some ways
> than the contour line you did while you were looking at your drawing."

Later practice (from the family letter):

> "Contour line drawing is a skill that one gets better at with practice. You could encourage your
> child to do more contour line drawings of complex organic shapes in your home – a pile of clothes, a
> plant, a pet (while it's sleeping)"

Materials listed: "White drawing paper: 9x12", two per student; Drawing pencils: 2H, 4B, 4H, HB, and
2B; Vinyl erasers; black Sharpies: ultra fine tip".

### VMFA, "Sketching to Learn: Contour Drawing" (VMFA, archived only)

> "A contour line is a continuous unbroken line that defines the outside shape of an object."

> "Look carefully at the outside shape of an object, and follow it carefully with your eyes.
> Now, without looking back down at your paper, draw the contour. Don't think about the object you are
> drawing; just allow your pencil to follow the same slow movement of your eyes as they focus on the
> contour. Your line should show every little bump, dip or turn. If your lines are smooth and straight,
> you are not going slowly enough!
> Don't worry about what your drawing looks like; it may not be recognizable! That's okay. It should
> look a little funny!"

> 2. Focus your eye on some part of the object and begin moving your pencil to sketch what your eyes

### Research: Chamberlain and Wagemans 2016 (CW), Cohen 2005 (CO), and R03

Why people draw symbols (CW, p. 13 of the accepted version):

> "The evidence on delusions of vision that follows is split into negative categorical schemas, which
> distort the drawn image by replacing perceptual content with labels or canonical percepts, and
> positive pictorial schemas, which help the artist to segment the visual scene in systematic ways that
> aid depiction."

> "This suggests that the development of artistic expertise involves an ability to see familiar object
> and scenes as if they were unfamiliar, in order to reduce the influence of biasing categorical
> schemas."

Nicolaïdes, quoted in CW (p. 23), the closest this file gets to the original:

> "As Kimon Nicolaides (1941) notes in his instructional book on representational drawing, "With the
> exception of the contour study, there is no drawing that is not a memory drawing because, no matter
> how slight the interval is from the time you look at the model until you look at your drawing or
> painting, you are memorizing what you have just seen". (p. 40)"

Looking often (CW, p. 23, and CO's abstract):

> "In a causal study, Cohen (2005) found that increasing the rate of gaze shifting between drawing and
> to-be-drawn object increased drawing accuracy, suggesting that reduced reliance on visual memory leads
> to more accurate drawing." (CW)

> "Experiment 1 revealed a positive relation between gaze frequency and drawing accuracy (r2=.33).
> Experiments 2, 3, and 4 demonstrated that gaze frequency directly influences drawing accuracy." (CO)

R03, section A5, the lines that bear on this lesson:

> "**Why people can't draw what they see.** Comparing tracing with freehand drawing, motor coordination
> was "a very minimal source" of error. The main source was misperception of the object"

> "**Attention and schemas, not "seeing more truly."** [...] The review concludes that what matters is
> "domain-specific, flexible, top-down control of visual attention": suppressing wrong category
> schemas, using helpful pictorial schemas, and segmenting the scene well"

> "**Upside-down drawing.** Drawing from an inverted image had no effect in one study and only a small
> one in another"

> "Looking back and forth between model and drawing more often increased accuracy (Cohen 2005, same
> review)."

The phrase "look little, look often" is not in A5. It is in `research/02-drawing-curricula-survey.md`,
section 4: "Frequent glances go with accuracy: "Look little, look often"
(https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193626)." That DOI is CO, read above.

## 3. Checks a learner can see on the page

Quoted from the sources:

- "Your line should show every little bump, dip or turn. If your lines are smooth and straight, you are
  not going slowly enough!" (VMFA). The one numeric-free but visible test of speed.
- "laying in all the little turns and bumps" (CP-V)
- "in a continuous line. Do not lift your pencil" (RISD); "NEVER LIFT YOUR PENCIL/PEN OFF THE PAPER"
  (UA)
- "Can you find any place on your buddy's drawing where it looks like s/he might have lost
  concentration, and made something up?" (AI; a peer check that works as a self-check)
- "Where do you see his/her contour line describing the inner and outer edges of the form well?" (AI)
- Assessment criteria: "Records the inner and outer edges of a complex form from observation" and
  "Records the inner and outer edges of a complex form without looking at his/her drawing" (AI)
- "Which is the most truthful to the form and character of your shoe?" (AI, comparing the two drawings)
- What not to judge: "it may not be recognizable! That's okay. It should look a little funny!" (VMFA);
  "they look odd and whimsical" (MFM); "Blind contour drawing may not produce a lifelike drawing" (WP);
  "it is not about making a pretty picture" (UA)
- "emphasize filling the whole page with one drawing" (MFM; a size check)

## 4. Terms, in the sources' own words

- **Contour.** "a linear approach to drawing, focusing on creating descriptive outlines" (CP).
  "A contour line is a continuous unbroken line that defines the outside shape of an object." (VMFA).
  "Contour is described as the outside or inside edge of an object." (MFM). "Contour lines show the
  inner and outer edges of a form." (AI). The sources differ on inside edges; see section 5.
- **Contour line drawing.** "slow analytical drawing to capture the outline of a shape" (CP-V).
- **Contour seeing.** "really what you're practicing here is contour seeing it's looking at the specific
  shape of an object and putting it on your paper" (CP-V).
- **Symbol.** "this is a symbol of a screwdriver because when i drew this i didn't have any reference so
  this is my generalized sense of all the screwdrivers i've ever seen" (CP-V); "Beginners often fall
  into the trap of drawing 'symbols' instead of actual objects" (CP); "memorized drawing symbols" (WP).
  The research term is "negative categorical schemas, which distort the drawn image by replacing
  perceptual content with labels or canonical percepts" (CW).
- **Blind contour drawing.** "a drawing exercise, where an artist draws the contour of a subject without
  looking at the paper" (WP); "the artist stares directly at the drawing subject and never looks at the
  paper" (MFM).
- **Pure contour drawing.** Betty Edwards' name for blind contour (WP).
- **Continuous line drawing.** "essentially an outline, where the artist sketches a picture without
  lifting the drawing tool" (TMA).
- **Cross contour.** Named but not defined by UA ("regular or cross contour"). Drawabox uses "contour
  lines" for something else: "Contour lines are the marks that run along the surface of an object. In
  doing so, they provide our eyes and brain with valuable information describing just how those surfaces
  themselves flow through space." (DB-2). That is lesson 10's meaning. Lesson 3 should not use
  "contour lines" in Drawabox's sense, or lesson 10 will collide with it.
- **Modified contour.** No source read here defines it. The only mention is a reader comment under CP
  ("2. Modified Contour (only look at paper when you lift pencil or change directions)", Rob Levin,
  describing a college class), which is not a citable source. AI's looked contour ("Look at the thing
  you are drawing MUCH more than at your paper") is the citable stand-in.

## 5. Proposal (not source text; every element cites its quote)

### Steps (5)

1. **Set up.** Put the subject beside the page and the pen on the page. Pick a starting point on the
   subject's edge ("Place your pencil/pen on your paper • Look at your non-dominant hand and pick a
   starting point", UA; "Focus your vision on the top of your object, and put your pencil at the top of
   your paper", AI).
2. **Track the edge with your eyes.** Move your eyes slowly along the edge and move the pen at the same
   speed ("Slowly track the edge with your eyes while drawing what you see", RISD; "move your eyes
   slowly down the object while your drawing hand moves at the same speed", AI; "like an ant traveling
   along the edge of the object", UA).
3. **Keep your eyes off the page.** Do not look down and do not lift the pen until you reach the start
   again ("Do not lift your pencil or look at the paper", RISD; "NEVER EVER LOOK AT YOUR PAPER", UA).
   Cover the drawing hand if you peek ("drape a light cloth or large paper over your drawing hand", UBC).
4. **Draw every bump.** Record each dent, crease and turn you see; a smooth stretch means you sped up
   ("Your line should show every little bump, dip or turn. If your lines are smooth and straight, you
   are not going slowly enough!", VMFA; "laying in all the little turns and bumps", CP-V).
5. **Draw it again, looking mostly at the subject.** Turn the subject to a new position and draw it
   with glances at the page, keeping most of your looking on the subject ("Look at the thing you are
   drawing MUCH more than at your paper", AI; "placed in a new position", AI). If you lose the edge,
   go back to the last part you drew with attention and carry on from there ("go back to the place on
   your drawing where you can see that you were still concentrating", AI).

Credit line candidates: "Ctrl+Paint calls this contour seeing" (CP-V). For the blind form, credit
Nicolaïdes through WP if a name is wanted.

### The usual mistake (one pair)

- **Wrong:** a smooth, tidy outline of "a hand" or "a shoe": rounded fingers of equal length, a
  cartoon sole. It is drawn from memory.
- **Right:** a slower, bumpier line with this hand's knuckle creases and this shoe's stitching and
  scuffs, even where proportions go wrong.
- **Reason (CP):** "The trick is to draw the unique contour you're observing, and not a generalized
  outline drawn from your memory." Backed by CP-V's screwdriver ("this is a symbol of a screwdriver")
  and VMFA's "If your lines are smooth and straight, you are not going slowly enough!"

### Main exercise

- **What:** your non-drawing hand and one of your shoes (COURSE.md; hand: UA, UBC, DIS-38; shoe: AI).
- **How many:** three of each, re-posing the hand and turning the shoe each time (COURSE.md says three;
  "Re-pose your hand", UBC; "placed in a new position", AI). Suggested order per subject: two blind,
  then one looking mostly at the subject (AI does looked then blind; UA does normal then blind; no
  source fixes the order, so the writer can choose, but say "blind first" only as a choice).
- **How long:** sources disagree and none times a shoe. Sourced figures: 5 minutes per fist (DIS-38:
  "a total of two fists", "10 minutes"); 1 minute per hand (UA); 30 s, 1 min, 3 min (UBC); 10 minutes
  for the longest blind drawing (UBC landscape). Proposal: 3 minutes per hand drawing (UBC's longest)
  and 5 minutes per shoe drawing (DIS-38's rate), about 25 minutes in all. Test it (WRITING.md step
  5) before shipping, since the shoe time is borrowed from a fist exercise.
- **With what:** ballpoint pen or fineliner, as in lesson 1, no eraser ("erasers are not used", MFM;
  "Don't bother to erase", AI). Fill the page: one drawing per half page at least ("filling the whole
  page with one drawing", MFM).

### Checks (5)

1. Each blind drawing is one continuous line, with no gaps where the pen lifted ("continuous line", "Do
   not lift your pencil", RISD).
2. The line shows small bumps and turns along its whole length, with no long smooth curves ("every
   little bump, dip or turn", VMFA).
3. You can point to three details that belong to this hand or this shoe, not to hands or shoes in
   general ("all sorts of details that you never noticed before", CP-V; "Only draw what you see", AI).
   The number three is the writer's choice and must be tested; no source gives one.
4. In the looked drawing, inside edges are drawn too: creases, the shoe's opening, the laces ("Records
   the inner and outer edges of a complex form from observation", AI; "the openings at the top of the
   shoes", DIS-54).
5. You can find the places where you stopped looking and made the shape up ("lost concentration, and
   made something up", AI).

What not to check, said once in the lesson: whether the blind drawings look like the subject ("it may
not be recognizable! That's okay.", VMFA; "may not produce a lifelike drawing", WP). Lesson 1's
smooth-line checks do not apply here: a wobble that follows the edge is correct.

### Practise later (4)

1. **Blind contour warm-up** · 3 to 5 minutes before a session, any object ("Some artists use contour
   drawing to warm up", WP; "similar to sporting activity warm up exercises", MFM).
2. **Ten objects, slowly** · ten simple objects, one careful contour each, across several sittings
   ("pick 10 relatively simple objects [...] these should take a while this is not a speed assignment",
   CP-V).
3. **Timed hands** · blind, your re-posed hand at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 3 minutes on one sheet (UBC).
4. **Turn it and draw it again** · one object, outline, turn it slightly, outline again, several times
   ("360° Drawing", RISD series). Also suits lesson 16 later.

Other sourced options: the fist without lifting the pencil, two fists in 10 minutes (DIS-37);
contour drawings of "a pile of clothes, a plant, a pet (while it's sleeping)" (AI).

### Where the sources disagree

- **Look at the paper or not.** Never look: WP, RISD, VMFA, MFM, UA, UBC, TMA. Look, after a light
  layout: CP-V. Look "MUCH more" at the subject than the paper: AI. Split attention "equally": DIS-5.
  The research favours frequent glances for accuracy (CO; CW: "increasing the rate of gaze shifting
  [...] increased drawing accuracy"). The proposal uses blind drawing as the looking drill and a looked
  drawing for accuracy, which fits all of them.
- **One slow line or many light ones.** WP, RISD, VMFA: one slow continuous line. DIS-3: a slow single
  perimeter line "is easy to get off track" and "may have a tendency to look wiggly"; it prefers many
  light quick lines to "feel out" a shape. DIS-3 is teaching a finished outline, not a looking drill;
  the lesson should say the blind line is meant to wander.
- **Outline only, or inside edges too.** VMFA "outside shape"; CP-V "only draw the very outline". MFM
  "outside or inside edge"; AI "inner and outer edges". The proposal keeps the blind drawings to the
  edge you are tracking and asks for inside edges in the looked drawing.
- **Lift the pen?** RISD and UA: never. AI: "Oftentimes [...] won't pick up his/her pencil" (a habit,
  not a rule), and restart from the last good point if concentration slips.
- **Time.** From 30 seconds (UBC) to "should take a while" (CP-V); no source times a shoe.
- **Why it works.** WP gives Nicolaïdes (sight and touch) and Edwards (a "shift from left mode to right
  mode"). Delineare dropped right-brain framing (`COURSE.md`, "What was cut"; R03 on upside-down
  drawing). Use the symbol explanation (CP, WP's "memorized drawing symbols", CW's categorical schemas)
  and leave the hemisphere claim out.
