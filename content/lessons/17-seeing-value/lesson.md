# Seeing value

Value is how light or dark something is, whatever its colour. In this lesson you'll make a value scale and learn to squint at a subject until it breaks into three flat shapes. Drawing those shapes first gives every later shading lesson a plan to follow.

![A page with a five-box value scale along the top and a mug below it drawn as three flat shapes: bare paper, one mid tone and one dark.](example)

## Steps

1. **Make a scale.** Rule a strip of five boxes. Leave the first as paper and fill the last with the darkest tone your pencil makes. Fill the three between so each is one even step darker than the last.

   ![A strip of five boxes from white to near black, each a step darker than the one before.](scale)

2. **Squint.** Set a mug or a shoe in front of you by a window. Squint until the detail blurs. Compare the darkest value with the lightest, and look for the plain shapes they make.

   ![A mug with soft gradations on the left, and the same mug squinted down to a few flat shapes on the right.](squint)

3. **Draw the borders.** Draw only the edges between the shapes you see: the light, the medium and the dark.

   ![A mug drawn as outlines only: the lit side, the middle, the shadow side, the open top, the handle and the table shadow.](borders)

4. **Fill in three values.** Dow works with three values: light, medium and dark. Leave the lightest shapes as paper and fill the medium and dark shapes each with one even tone.

   ![The same mug filled with bare paper, one even mid tone and one even dark.](fill)

5. **Compare.** Match each tone to your scale, then squint at the page and at the subject in turn. Proko suggests flipping the drawing, using a mirror or leaning back to see it fresh.

   ![The flat mug with its three tones linked to the matching boxes on the value scale.](compare)

Proko teaches the squint and the value scale, and Dow the three values.

## Common mistake

![Left, a white mug filled white all over so its shadow side is lost. Right, the mug filled with the tones seen while squinting.](mistake)

The most common mistake is filling a shape by what the object is: a white mug, so white. Proko calls an object's own lightness its local value, and says the light on it matters as much. Fill what you see while squinting.

## Checking your page

Look at the page and at the subject while squinting, and check these:

1. Each box on your scale is darker than the one before.
2. The drawing uses three values, each filled evenly.
3. The lightest shapes are bare paper.
4. The dark shapes sit where they sit in the subject.

A tone looks lighter or darker depending on its neighbours, so judge each one against the shapes beside it.
