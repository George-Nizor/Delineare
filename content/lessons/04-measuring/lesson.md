# Measuring

Ctrl+Paint says drawing is at least half seeing. In this lesson you'll learn to measure proportions and angles with a pencil held at arm's length. You can then compare the parts of a subject before you draw them.

![A page with a mug and a chair, each with its measured ratio written beside it.](example)

## Steps

1. **Hold the pencil.** Close one eye and stretch your arm straight out with the elbow locked. A bent elbow brings the pencil closer and enlarges every measurement. Keep the pencil upright, not tipped toward or away from you.

   ![Eye, straight arm and upright pencil, with sight lines running past the pencil to the top and bottom of a mug.](hold)

2. **Take a unit.** Line the pencil tip up with the top of one part, such as the mug's handle, and slide your thumb to its bottom. Keep your thumb still. That span is your unit.

   ![The pencil tip on the top of a mug's handle and the thumb on its bottom, with the span marked as the unit.](unit)

3. **Compare.** Hold the unit against another part and count how many fit. Draw the same ratio on paper. Proko calls this comparative measuring: the size on paper can differ, but the relationships must match.

   ![A chair marked off in three equal steps of the mug's unit.](compare)

4. **Sight an angle.** Hold the pencil along the edge you're measuring, square to your line of sight. Compare it with a vertical or horizontal, which are easier to judge than a slant.

   ![A pencil laid along a leaning edge beside a vertical reference, and the same lean drawn on the page.](angle)

5. **Check the page.** Hold the pencil against a part of your drawing and mark its length with a finger. Keep the finger still and compare it with the larger part on the subject. The ratio on paper should match.

   ![A mug and a smaller drawing of it, both measuring the same height against width.](check)

Proko teaches this method, and Drawing Academy describes the grip. Harold Speed's 1913 manual does the same job with a knitting needle.

## Common mistake

![Left, a squat mug drawn over the true outline because the pencil said so. Right, the same mug changed because it looked wrong.](mistake)

Many learners trust the pencil over their eyes. Proko warns that a millimetre of error at arm's length becomes inches on the page, and says that if something looks wrong, it is wrong. Estimate by eye first, measure second, and change the drawing whenever the two disagree.

## Checking your page

Take a photo of the mug and chair from where you sat, and hold it next to your drawing:

1. The mug's height-to-width ratio matches the photo.
2. The chair's seat is the same fraction of its height.
3. Angled edges lean the same way and by about the same amount.
4. The gaps between parts are in the same places.

Photos flatten depth, so judge proportions, not shading.
