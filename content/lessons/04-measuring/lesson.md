# Measuring

Ctrl+Paint puts it this way: drawing is at least half seeing. In this lesson you'll learn to measure proportions and angles with a pencil held at arm's length. You can then compare parts of a subject before you draw them.

## Steps

1. **Hold the pencil.** Close one eye and stretch your arm straight out with the elbow locked. A bent elbow brings the pencil closer and makes every measurement larger. Keep the pencil upright, not tipped toward or away from you.

2. **Take a unit.** Line the pencil tip up with the top of one part, such as a mug's handle, and slide your thumb to its bottom. Keep your thumb still. That span is your unit.

3. **Compare.** Hold the unit against another part and count how many fit. Draw the same ratio on paper. Proko calls this comparative measuring: the size can differ, but the relationships must match.

4. **Sight an angle.** Hold the pencil along the edge you're measuring, still perpendicular to your line of sight. Compare it with an imaginary vertical or horizontal, which are easier to judge than a slant.

5. **Check the page.** Mark your drawing's smaller measurement on the pencil with a finger, then compare it with the larger one on the subject. The ratio on paper should match.

Proko teaches this method, and Drawing Academy describes the pencil grip.

## Common mistake

Many learners trust the pencil over their eyes. Proko warns that small measuring errors add up. His rule: "if something looks wrong, then it's wrong." Estimate by eye first, measure second, and change the drawing whenever the two disagree.

## Checking your page

Take a photo of the mug and chair from where you sat, and hold it next to your drawing:

1. The mug's height-to-width ratio matches the photo.
2. The chair's seat is the same fraction of its height.
3. Angled edges lean the same way and by about the same amount.
4. The gaps between parts are in the same places.

Photos flatten depth, so judge proportions, not shading.
