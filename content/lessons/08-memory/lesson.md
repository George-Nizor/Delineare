# Drawing from memory

Drawing from imagination needs a store of things you've seen. In this lesson you'll learn a loop for building that store: study a simple object, draw it with the object out of sight, then compare and correct. It also shows you which parts you didn't look at closely.

![A mug, a first drawing from memory with the proportions and handle wrong, and a second drawing that fixes them.](example)

## Steps

1. **Study.** Choose a simple object and look at it for about 3 minutes. Note the proportions of its main lines and their shapes, as if you were drawing it in your head.

   ![An eye studying a mug, with the angles and shapes noted.](study)

2. **Draw from memory.** Put the object away, out of sight. Draw it for about 5 minutes. Gaps in your drawing show what you didn't take in.

   ![A mug hidden behind a screen, and on the page only the rough drawing of what was remembered.](memory)

3. **Compare.** Bring the object back. Put it beside your drawing and note each difference, in proportion, shape and detail.

   ![A mug and a drawing of it, with level lines showing the drawing is taller and its handle sits higher.](compare)

4. **Draw again.** Hide the object again and draw it a second time, fixing what you found.

   ![The first drawing faded beside a second drawing that corrects its height and handle.](again)

Proko teaches this loop in exercises 4 and 5 of his visual memory lesson. Harold Speed, writing in 1913, recommends starting with simple things and making a memory drawing in your sketchbook every day.

## Common mistake

![Left, an eye looking at the mug while drawing from memory. Right, an eye on the page with the mug out of sight.](mistake)

Looking at the object while you draw from memory feels like help, but it skips the work of recalling. Look only before you draw and when you compare. Proko says to check the reference only after each drawing.

## Checking your page

Set the object beside your second drawing:

1. Its overall shape matches the object's.
2. You fixed the biggest difference from your first drawing.
3. Parts you missed the first time are now drawn.
4. You looked at the object only before and after drawing.

Early memory drawings come out rough. Speed says to keep going and draw as clearly as you can.
