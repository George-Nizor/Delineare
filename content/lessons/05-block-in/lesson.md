# Block-in and negative space

In this lesson you'll learn to start a drawing with the big straight-edged shape around the subject. Then you draw the empty spaces around and between its parts. Ctrl+Paint says that sometimes the best way to see an object is to look at the space around it.

![A chair drawn through its gaps, inside a faint dashed envelope.](example)

## Steps

1. **See one shape.** Look at the whole subject, or a group of objects, as a single shape. Squint to lose the detail.

   ![A chair beside the same chair squinted into one flat shape.](see)

2. **Mark the extremes.** Lightly dot the highest, lowest, widest and narrowest points. Get Realism says accuracy matters more than the number of dots.

   ![A chair with a dot at each outer corner, labelled highest, lowest and widest.](extremes)

3. **Join them with straight lines.** Sight the angle between two dots with your pencil and draw it as a straight line, even where the real edge curves. Call the outline you get the **envelope**.

   ![The dots joined by straight lines around the chair.](envelope)

4. **Check the envelope.** Measure its height against its width on the subject and on your page. If the page is too tall or too wide, move the lines now.

   ![Two envelopes of different sizes, each with height measuring 1.5 times its width.](check)

5. **Draw the gaps.** The space around and between parts is **negative space**. Draw the largest gap first, such as the opening under a chair seat, then the smaller ones. Check each gap's angles and proportions.

   ![The gaps of a chair numbered from largest to smallest.](gaps)

6. **Cut the corners.** Break the envelope into smaller angles, and round them into curves only once the proportions hold.

   ![An envelope with its corners cut off, and beside it the same shape rounded.](corners)

Ctrl+Paint teaches the envelope and negative space as ways to see. Sciandra's chair exercise supplies the gap drawing, and Harold Speed describes the same scaffold of straight lines in 1913.

## Common mistake

![Left, a chair built part by part that has grown past its box. Right, a chair drawn inside its envelope that stays within it.](mistake)

Starting with a detail, such as one chair leg, leaves nothing to judge it against, so drawings built part by part grow out of proportion. Draw the whole envelope first, then divide it. Ctrl+Paint calls this working from basic to complex.

## Checking your page

Look at your chair:

1. The envelope's height and width match the chair's.
2. The largest gap is the right shape and in the right place.
3. The gaps between legs are drawn, not the legs.
4. Straight lines come before curves.
